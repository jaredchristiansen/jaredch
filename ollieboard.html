02
Problem Definition

Restaurants don't know
how many hours they actually need.

Owners set their schedules based on feel — what they think they'll need. But no one has mapped every task required to run a store properly. The result is either wasted hours or an understaffed shift that underperforms.

The Scheduling Gap

Owners schedule by intuition, not data. Without knowing the real work volume, every schedule is a guess — some days overstaffed, others dangerously thin.

The Hidden Task Load

Every store has hundreds of recurring tasks — dough management, sanitation, restocks — that no one has ever catalogued or sequenced. They happen, or they don't.

The Margin Leak

Inefficiency at this level isn't visible on any dashboard. Owners lose margin to problems they can't quantify — which means they can't fix them either.

"The schedule is a guess. We wanted to make it a science."

OllieBoard core thesis
The Opportunity

Catalogue every task a store needs. Map it, sequence it, time it. Build the real schedule — not what someone thinks the store needs, but what it actually requires to run at its best.

The Vision

An operations layer that works like an intelligent manager: knowing what needs to happen, when, and whether it did. Saving money and increasing store quality without adding headcount.

03
Discovery & Validation

In the store
before the screen.

Before designing anything, I spent weeks inside the problem space — stretching dough alongside operators, running workshops with top franchise owners, and conducting 30+ discovery interviews. The goal wasn't to validate a solution. It was to understand what was actually breaking.

Being trained by the owner of a top-10 Domino's franchise — who walks us through every aspect of his operations.

Going Deep

Discovery doesn't happen behind a computer. To understand the real task load in a store, we had to get hands on. The owner of a top-10 Domino's franchise gave us a full operational breakdown — every station, every cycle, every decision his employees don't even know is being made.

That kind of access only happens when you show up. And it surfaced constraints no remote interview would have caught.

The Shortcut That Saved a Month

Before building the scheduling system, we tested it manually. Instead of writing code, we went into a store and assigned tasks ourselves — mimicking what the software would eventually do. Within a day, we surfaced structural flaws that would have taken weeks to uncover in production.

What We Hypothesized
Employees need a checklist Screen-based guidance works Task completion = checkbox Managers will supervise
What We Learned
Employees pencil-whip tasks Phones = distraction + hygiene risk Trust must be verified, not assumed Motion is the real signal
04
Building the System

Designing for a
physical world.

The interface was only one layer. Below it: a task optimization engine, a sensor network, hardware I sourced and installed myself, and algorithms that turn radar data into employee position.

The Sensor Layer

When we realized self-reported task completion couldn't be relied upon, we researched, sourced, and programmed a network of mmWave radar sensors. The algorithms I wrote convert raw radar signal into employee position — so the system knows what's actually happening.

The full sensor set — branded, programmed, and ready for installation across the store.

OllieBoard running live at a Domino's pilot location.

Live in Store

The first OllieBoard screen mounted and running in a real Domino's location. Designed for back-of-house — loud, fast, no time to learn a new interface. Every design decision was made with that wall in mind.

Layer 01 — Intelligence

Task Optimization Engine

Built the core algorithm that ingests every store task — dough management, sanitation, restocks — and surfaces the right job to the right person at the right moment. The foundation for real data-driven scheduling.

Layer 02 — Interface

Workstation Displays

Designed for a demanding environment: fast pace, language barriers, aging hardware, employees reading mid-task. Built in Bubble, deployed to mounted screens. Legibility and speed were the only metrics that mattered.

Layer 03 — Verification

mmWave Sensor Network

Sourced, programmed, assembled, and physically installed sensors in pilot stores. Wrote position-tracking algorithms that turn radar signal into actionable data — ground truth on whether work is actually being done.

Layer 04 — Ops

Supporting Infrastructure

Automated schedule scraping, Domino's data parsing, Discord integrations, AI-assisted training workflows, pitch deck, investor materials, intern management, ERDs, full product specs.

05
What I Learned

The hard lessons
are the real work.

Zero-to-one is a constant negotiation between building and learning. The moments I was most wrong are the ones I grew from most.

⊘

Don't trust self-reported completion

Employees checked off tasks they hadn't done — "pencil whipping." This single insight reshaped the entire sensor strategy and verification layer of the product.

⊘

Resist the "just one more thing" trap

At an early startup, scope creep fragments your learning. Every feature added before validating the core is debt against clarity.

✓

Proving value manually beats building first

The manual in-store test was the highest-leverage thing we did. One afternoon in a Domino's saved us a month of development. Test the hypothesis, not the software.

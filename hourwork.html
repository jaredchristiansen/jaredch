HourWork Dashboard

2021 – 2023 ∙ UX design & bubble.io development

HourWork helps restaurant owners and operators (e.g., McDonald’s, Domino’s) engage their hourly workforce through automated messaging and surveys. Managers can trigger milestone messages, collect employee feedback, and re-engage past applicants to support hiring. The platform is designed for franchise owners managing multiple stores and teams at scale.

Context

Problem

As HourWork’s automated messaging and surveys scaled across more locations and use cases, managers lacked visibility into what was happening across their teams.

Store owners needed a way to quickly understand employee engagement across locations.

Solution & impact

A single dashboard that helps managers understand what messages are being sent, what’s coming up, and how employees are responding across locations.

Understand

Understand

Initial problem discovery

Before designing anything, I needed to understand how the system was actually being used, and where it was falling short for managers.

I started by talking with the team and reviewing the questions coming in from customers. A clear pattern showed up quickly: managers weren’t confused about how to send messages, they were confused about what the platform was doing on their behalf.

The challenge was made clearer

This wasn’t just a missing dashboard — it was a visibility gap that limited trust and made the product hard to use. My focus at this stage was defining what managers needed to understand at a glance in order to feel confident using the platform on their own, and what information mattered most to support that change.

Research

Research

What we found about our users

Once the problem space was clearer, the next step was pressure-testing our assumptions—specifically, who this dashboard was actually for and how it would be used in real life.

Most managers were accessing HourWork on a shared computer in the back of the store, usually between tasks or during short breaks. They weren’t especially tech-savvy, and they weren’t interested in learning a complex system. That context made the priority obvious: the dashboard needed to be fast to read, predictable, and hard to mess up. Visual flair mattered far less than clarity.

Alongside this, I looked at how other products approached similar problems. Rather than reinventing the wheel, I reviewed dashboards and management tools that successfully surfaced activity, status, and history at a glance. This helped identify familiar patterns managers would already recognize, as well as common pitfalls to avoid, such as overly dense layouts or excessive configuration.

That work reinforced a key constraint: this dashboard wasn’t a place to show off. It needed to stay out of the way and let managers get what they needed quickly. Those insights directly shaped both the structure and tone of the designs that followed.

Explore

Wireframing

Wireframes are where ideas get pressure-tested. I use them to explore multiple directions quickly, challenge assumptions, and understand the tradeoffs between clarity, flexibility, and complexity before committing to a solution.

The Complex / Power-User Dashboard

Why we thought it might work

This direction prioritized flexibility and completeness. With filters, detailed charts, and full activity logs, it aimed to give managers maximum visibility and control across stores. On the surface, it felt robust and future-proof — a dashboard that could grow with the product.

Why it failed

While powerful, this approach assumed time, technical comfort, and a desire for analysis that didn’t reflect how managers actually used the platform. It required configuration before delivering clarity and introduced unnecessary cognitive load. Research showed that managers needed quick reassurance — not a reporting tool.

Takeaway: Optimized for depth over speed.

The Notification-Heavy / Activity Feed

Why we thought it might work

This direction centered on a real-time activity feed, surfacing every message, survey, and response in chronological order. It felt dynamic and transparent — giving managers a sense of constant visibility into what the system was doing.

Why it failed

While transparent, this approach over-indexed on recency. The volume of activity quickly became noisy, making it difficult to identify meaningful patterns or upcoming milestones. Instead of reducing uncertainty, it created distraction. Managers needed structured clarity, not a live stream of events.

Takeaway: Too noisy for fast decision-making.

The final structure

This layout separated short-term and longer-term information, reduced cognitive load, and created a clear scanning hierarchy. With this structure in place, the remaining work shifted from deciding where things belonged to refining how they should be presented.

Prototype

Prototype

First designs

With the overall layout established, the next step was refining how each section communicated information. We knew what needed to be shown — company-wide statistics, today’s touchpoints, weekly activity, and locations — but the challenge was determining the most readable and useful way to present it.

At this stage, the focus shifted from structure to clarity. I explored multiple variations for each module, testing different levels of detail, grouping strategies, and visual hierarchy. Many of these options were intentionally ruled out when they introduced unnecessary density or required too much interpretation from the user.

The goal wasn’t to show more information — it was to make the right information easier to understand at a glance.

Assemble the design, move to testing

Validate

Validate

Test with users

With a structured prototype in place, the next step was putting it in front of managers to test the assumptions behind the design. The goal wasn’t aesthetic feedback — it was clarity.

We focused on questions like:

  • Can they quickly understand what’s happening across their stores?
  • Do they know where to click without instruction?
  • Does the hierarchy match how they think about their day?

These sessions surfaced small friction points and confirmed which areas felt intuitive. Addressing this feedback before development ensured the final design reduced uncertainty rather than introducing new complexity.
